4. THE SIX ALGORITHMS — 3:15–10:55

4.1 LINEAR REGRESSION — "How much yield can we expect?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------

[OPEN /linear-regression.]

Linear Regression is our yield-estimation model. It learns a weighted
relationship between field conditions and historical yield, then estimates
a number — here, tonnes per hectare.

The data is CYCleSS: field-level records from 2015 to 2017, covering five
crops. For each field we use Sentinel-1 radar summaries from three signals
— Ratio, VH, and VV — with mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and
observation count for each. Crop and year add context. That gives 742
training and 192 held-out fields.

[CLICK "LOAD EXAMPLE INPUTS", THEN "CALCULATE YIELD ESTIMATE".]

This oilseed rape example estimates about 6.0 tonnes per hectare. The
orange marker places it against the held-out fields, and the scatter plot
compares actual and predicted yield.

On the held-out set, R-squared is about 0.66 and mean absolute error is
1.27 tonnes per hectare. In plain language, the model explains about
two-thirds of the variation in yield, with a typical miss of 1.27 tonnes
per hectare. That supports planning, but it does not replace a field
measurement.

Why Linear Regression? It is transparent, fast, and a strong baseline.
Radar sees canopy structure and works through cloud cover. The page also
fills missing summaries from training medians and warns when inputs fall
outside the training range.


4.2 NAIVE BAYES — "What is the most likely disease, or the best crop?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------

[OPEN /naive-bayes.]

Naive Bayes is a probabilistic classifier. It applies Bayes' theorem and
assumes features are independent — a simplification, which is why it is
called "naive." It is fast, works with small data, and gives a probability
ranking.

We use it for two questions. Soybean disease screening uses 35 symptom
features, 15 disease classes, 290 training and 340 test records. Crop
recommendation uses seven soil and climate inputs for 22 crops, with 1,760
training and 440 test records.

[LOAD SOYBEAN EXAMPLE, THEN PREDICT.]

This held-out case is predicted as Diaporthe stem canker, and the "Why
this answer?" panel shows which symptoms contributed most. If I clear a
few symptoms, the probability spread widens — the model skips missing
features instead of crashing.

On the soybean test set, accuracy is 91.5% and top-three accuracy is
98.8%, against a 14.1% baseline. Even with half the symptoms hidden,
accuracy stays around 83.5%. Crop recommendation reaches 99.5%, but that
dataset is clean and separable, so it is not a field guarantee.

Why Naive Bayes? Disease screening often starts with incomplete,
categorical observations, and the probability ranking supports triage.


4.3 K-NEAREST NEIGHBORS — "What is this sample most similar to?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------

[OPEN /knn.]

K-Nearest Neighbors does not learn an equation. It stores known examples,
measures similarity, finds the k closest neighbors, and lets them vote.

We use it on three simulated datasets: soil type, with seven lab
measurements and four classes; soil fertility, with seven nutrient
measurements and three classes; and plant condition, with five canopy
measurements and four classes. The data is simulated but built from
realistic agronomic ranges, with overlapping classes and a little label
noise so the neighbors can disagree.

[LOAD EXAMPLE, THEN MOVE THE K SLIDER.]

Here is a soil-type example. The map is a two-dimensional PCA projection;
the new sample sits among its neighbors. The table lists the nearest
samples and distances, and the vote panel shows how the prediction changes
as I move k.

Held-out accuracy is 93.3% for soil type at k = 24, 92.6% for soil
fertility at k = 22, and 93.2% for plant condition at k = 13. The
baselines are 25.6%, 33.5%, and 25.3%, so the models are learning real
structure.

Why KNN? It mirrors how an agronomist reasons: "this soil looks like these
soils I have seen before." Small k is sensitive to noise; large k smooths
the boundary. We choose k by cross-validation, not by looking at the test
set.


4.4 SUPPORT VECTOR MACHINE — "Where is the boundary?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------

[OPEN /svm.]

A Support Vector Machine finds the boundary that separates classes with
the widest possible margin. The points closest to the boundary are the
support vectors. With a kernel, the boundary can bend to capture
non-linear patterns.

Our demo uses a simulated crop-disease dataset: 960 training and 240 test
records, four measurements — air temperature, humidity, leaf wetness, and
a red-edge stress index — and two classes, healthy and diseased. Diseased
crops sit in a middle temperature band with high humidity, while healthy
crops surround that band. A straight line cannot separate them well; a
curve can.

[TOGGLE LINEAR VS RBF, THEN MOVE C AND GAMMA.]

The shaded regions are the predicted zones, the line is the boundary, and
the circled points are the support vectors. The model is refitted on the
server each time.

On all four measurements, the linear SVM reached 87.5% held-out accuracy;
the RBF SVM reached 93.3%, with 254 support vectors. Cross-validation
agrees: about 88.8% versus 94.8%. The two-dimensional view may show a
different number because it uses only the two selected measurements — that
is intentional, and it shows how feature choice changes the problem.

Why SVM? It is strong on small, high-dimensional classification problems
and gives a clear geometric picture. C controls tolerance for mistakes;
gamma controls how far each point's influence reaches.


4.5 DECISION TREE — "Should I irrigate now, soon, wait, or hold?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------

[OPEN /decision-tree.]

A Decision Tree learns a flowchart from data. Each node asks a yes/no
question; each leaf gives a prediction. It is one of the most explainable
models we have.

The irrigation demo uses 1,600 simulated field conditions, split into
1,280 training and 320 test records. The inputs are soil moisture, rain in
the last three days, forecast rain, crop stage, and air temperature. The
four actions are irrigate now, irrigate soon, wait, or hold off. The data
includes sensor noise and a 3% label flip, so the tree has to learn the
underlying agronomy.

[LOAD EXAMPLE, THEN PREDICT.]

This held-out case is recommended as "Wait," and the panel shows the exact
rules the field followed — soil moisture above a threshold, rain forecast,
and crop stage. That is the explanation, not a post-hoc guess.

At depth five, the tree reaches 82.2% test accuracy against a 43.6%
baseline, with cross-validation around 80.1%. Soil moisture is the most
important feature at 49.2%, followed by recent rain at 19.3%, crop stage
at 17.9%, and forecast rain at 13.0%. The accuracy-by-depth chart shows
why we stopped at five: the gains flatten, and deeper trees add complexity
without adding much.

Why a Decision Tree? Irrigation advice has to be explainable and
auditable. A grower can see exactly why the system said "wait."


4.6 ARTIFICIAL NEURAL NETWORK — "What disease is in this leaf photo?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------

[OPEN /neural-network.]

Our final model is a convolutional neural network — a type of artificial
neural network designed for images. Instead of hand-coding "look for dark
spots," the network learns filters for edges, spots, and textures, then
combines them into class scores.

The data is a 4,500-image subset of PlantVillage, covering five tomato-leaf
classes: healthy, early blight, late blight, bacterial spot, and yellow
leaf curl. Each class has 900 images, split into 3,600 training and 900
test images. Every image is resized to 64 by 64 pixels.

[PICK A SAMPLE LEAF.]

Here is an unseen test leaf. The network predicts the class, shows a
confidence score for every class, and gives a short action note. The "as
the network sees it" panel is an occlusion heat map: it greys out one
patch at a time and shows which patches most affect the answer. That makes
the CNN less of a black box.

The model reaches 92.2% test accuracy and a macro F1 of 92.2%, against a
20% baseline. A plain fully connected network reached 84.7%, so convolution
adds about 7.5 percentage points. Most remaining errors are between early
and late blight — visually similar diseases — which is what the confusion
matrix shows.

Why a CNN? Leaf diagnosis is a visual task, and convolution exploits the
spatial structure of images. Uploaded images are processed in memory and
are not stored.


5. HOW THE SYSTEM IS BUILT — 10:55–12:10
--------------------------------------------------------------------

[OPTIONAL: SHOW THE PROJECT FOLDER OR A SIMPLE THREE-LAYER DIAGRAM.]

Under the hood, AgroPulse is a Python Flask application. The front end is
HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Five classical models use scikit-learn; the
neural network uses TensorFlow and Keras. Trained artifacts are loaded
when the server starts.

The project has three layers: data, training, and serving. The data layer
holds raw and processed datasets. The training layer contains reproducible
scripts that prepare the data and train each model. The serving layer is
the Flask app, which exposes page routes and JSON prediction endpoints.
Each algorithm is a separate module, so a failure in one demo does not
take down the rest of the site.

We build in responsible defaults. Inputs are validated on the client and
the server. Physical limits are enforced, and values outside the training
range produce warnings rather than silent extrapolation. Missing values
are imputed from training medians where appropriate. We use fixed seeds,
train-only preprocessing, and held-out test sets; for CYCleSS, the split
is by grid group, so the same field cannot appear in both training and
test.

What the system is not: it is not deployed, it does not connect to live
sensors, and it has not been validated on external farms. The datasets are
limited, and the simulated demos are labelled as simulations.

APPENDIX C — Q&A PREP
====================================================================

Answer in three parts: a direct answer, the evidence, then the caveat.
If you do not know something, say so and explain what you would check.

1. WHY LINEAR REGRESSION INSTEAD OF A MORE POWERFUL MODEL?
   Interpretability and data size. With 742 training fields and a need to
   explain each input's effect, a linear baseline is transparent and fast.
   A tree ensemble or gradient boosting model is a natural next step once
   we have more data.

2. WHAT DOES R-SQUARED = 0.66 ACTUALLY MEAN?
   The model explains about 66% of the variation in held-out yield. The
   other 34% is unexplained. MAE of 1.27 t/ha tells us the typical size of
   the error, which is often easier to interpret than R-squared alone.

3. HOW DID YOU PREVENT DATA LEAKAGE?
   Preprocessing was fit on the training split only. For CYCleSS, we held
   out entire grid groups, not random rows, so the same field could not
   appear in both sets. The test set stayed untouched until final
   evaluation.

4. WHY IS NAIVE BAYES ACCURACY SO HIGH ON CROP RECOMMENDATION?
   That dataset is relatively clean and separable, and the classes have
   distinct feature ranges. The 99.5% is a held-out result, but it is not a
   field guarantee. We report the 4.5% majority baseline to show how much
   of the gain is real.

5. WHAT HAPPENS IF SYMPTOMS ARE MISSING IN NAIVE BAYES?
   The model skips missing features rather than treating them as a
   category. With half the symptoms hidden, accuracy drops to about 83.5%,
   still well above the 14.1% baseline. The UI shows how the probability
   ranking changes.

6. HOW DID YOU CHOOSE K IN KNN?
   By cross-validation over k = 1 to 25 on the training data. The best
   values were 24 for soil type, 22 for soil fertility, and 13 for plant
   condition. We did not tune on the test set.

7. WHY DOES KNN USE PCA IN THE DEMO?
   The model uses all features. PCA is only for the two-dimensional
   visualization. It lets the user see the training points and the new
   sample in a plane while the prediction still uses the full feature
   space.

8. WHY IS RBF BETTER THAN LINEAR FOR SVM?
   The simulated disease pattern is non-linear: diseased cases sit in a
   middle temperature and humidity band, so a straight line cannot
   separate them. The RBF kernel bends the boundary and captures that
   structure.

9. WHAT DO C AND GAMMA DO?
   C controls the penalty for misclassification: a larger C fits the
   training data more tightly. Gamma controls how far each training point's
   influence reaches: a larger gamma creates a tighter, more wiggly
   boundary. Both are regularization levers.

10. WHY STOP THE DECISION TREE AT DEPTH FIVE?
    Depth five gave the best cross-validated accuracy in the range we
    tested and kept the tree readable. The accuracy-by-depth curve shows
    diminishing returns; deeper trees add complexity and risk overfitting
    without a meaningful gain.

11. HOW DO YOU INTERPRET FEATURE IMPORTANCE IN THE TREE?
    It is the reduction in impurity contributed by each feature across the
    tree. Soil moisture is dominant at 49.2%, followed by recent rain and
    crop stage. It is a global summary, not a causal claim.

12. WHY USE A CNN INSTEAD OF A PLAIN NEURAL NETWORK?
    Images have spatial structure. Convolutional layers share weights
    across patches, so they learn local patterns like spots and edges with
    far fewer parameters. The plain network reached 84.7%; the CNN reached
    92.2%.

13. WHAT ARE THE CNN'S MAIN FAILURE CASES?
    Most confusion is between early and late blight, which look similar.
    The confusion matrix shows that. The confidence score and heat map help
    flag uncertain cases, but a lab test is still the definitive diagnosis.

14. HOW DO YOU KNOW THE MODELS ARE NOT OVERFITTING?
    We use held-out test sets, cross-validation for hyperparameters, and
    majority baselines. We also show training curves and per-class metrics.
    The gap between training and test is visible on the ANN page; it is
    small but not zero.

15. WHY ARE SOME DATASETS SIMULATED?
    Some agricultural scenarios are hard to source with real, labelled,
    shareable data. Simulation lets us test the pipeline and make the
    algorithm's behaviour visible, and we label those demos clearly. The
    CYCleSS, soybean, crop-recommendation, and PlantVillage datasets come
    from real or public sources.

16. HOW IS THE WEBSITE BUILT?
    Flask serves the pages and JSON APIs. The front end is HTML, CSS, and
    JavaScript. scikit-learn handles five models, and TensorFlow/Keras
    handles the CNN. Trained artifacts are loaded at startup. The app runs
    locally on port 5051.

17. WHAT ABOUT PRIVACY AND UPLOADED IMAGES?
    Uploaded leaf images are processed in memory and are not saved. The app
    is local and does not send data to an external service. It accepts
    JPEG/PNG files up to 5 MB.

18. WHAT ARE THE BIGGEST LIMITATIONS?
    Limited datasets, no external validation, simulated data for three
    demos, no live sensor integration, and no deployment. The models are
    educational decision support, not advice. Distribution shift and local
    calibration are the next big questions.

19. HOW WOULD YOU DEPLOY THIS IN THE REAL WORLD?
    Containerize the Flask app, put it behind a production server, add
    authentication and a database, integrate sensor and weather APIs,
    monitor drift, and establish a retraining and human-review loop. Start
    with a pilot on a few fields.

20. WHY DOES THE SITE INCLUDE A SAVINGS CALCULATOR IF IT IS NOT A MODEL?
    It is explicitly a planning scenario, not a prediction. The user
    supplies the costs and the assumed reduction; the page only does the
    arithmetic. We labelled it clearly to avoid implying a guaranteed
    saving.

21. WHAT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN AI, MACHINE LEARNING, AND DEEP LEARNING?
    AI is the broad goal of machines doing intelligent tasks. Machine
    learning is a subset that learns from data. Deep learning is machine
    learning with multi-layer neural networks. Our CNN is deep learning;
    the other five models are classical machine learning.

22. WHY SIX ALGORITHMS INSTEAD OF ONE?
    The project brief asks for all six, and it is also a good design
    choice: different questions need different tools. Regression for a
    number, classification for a label, a tree for a rule, and a CNN for an
    image.

23. WHAT DID YOU PERSONALLY CONTRIBUTE?
    [Answer honestly. Name one concrete piece of work — data preparation,

APPENDIX F — DELIVERY AND BACKUP TIPS
====================================================================

DELIVERY
- Speak to the audience, not the screen. Point, then look back at them.
- Do not read every number. Say the headline, then interpret it.
- Pause for one beat after a metric. Let it land.
- Use the same phrase for the three capabilities each time: yield
  estimation, condition screening, resource guidance.
- If you make a mistake, correct it briefly and move on. Do not apologize
  repeatedly.
- Keep water nearby. Slow down when you feel nervous.

BACKUP PLAN
- If Flask fails, switch to the saved screenshots and keep the explanation
  moving. Say: "I'll use the saved view so we can keep going."
- If TensorFlow is slow, the ANN page may take a few seconds. Open it
  before you start, or use a screenshot.
- If the internet fails, the site still works; only Google Fonts fall back.
- If port 5051 is busy, stop the old server with Ctrl+C and restart.
- If a demo returns an error, check that the trained models and processed
  data folders are present. The error message names the missing artifact.

WHAT NOT TO SAY
- Do not claim the models are deployed or field-validated.
- Do not claim the savings calculator is a model prediction.
- Do not claim 99.5% crop accuracy means real-world perfection.
- Do not claim the CNN replaces a plant pathologist.
- Do not apologize for using simpler models. Explain why they were chosen.
